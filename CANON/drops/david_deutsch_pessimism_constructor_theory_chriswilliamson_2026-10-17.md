## 2. THE VOCABULARY AUDIT: does the house's Constructor-Theory usage track Deutsch's formalism?

### 2.1 What Constructor Theory actually says (primary text, fetched this session)

- [E] **Core.** "Constructor theory seeks to express all fundamental scientific theories in terms of a dichotomy between possible and impossible physical transformations — those that can be caused to happen and those that cannot. This is a departure from the prevailing conception … to predict what will happen from initial conditions and laws of motion." (arXiv:1210.7439, abstract.)
- [E] **Objects.** The basic objects are **tasks**: input-to-output pairs of *attributes* (sets of states) of a **substrate**. A **constructor** is an entity that performs a task *and retains the ability to perform it again* (catalyst, heat engine, programmed factory). (constructortheory.org; 1210.7439 §1–2.)
- [E] **Impossible vs. possible.** A task is *impossible* if a law of physics forbids performing it to arbitrarily high accuracy. Otherwise it is *possible*, meaning it **can** be performed but **need not happen**. "Impossible tasks cannot be effected regardless of what knowledge is brought to bear. But if the right knowledge is applied, any possible task … can necessarily be effected" (§3.2).
- [E] **The role of negation.** "A central role for the impossible … a formal implementation of the Popperian idea that the content of a scientific theory is in what it forbids" (§3.6). And: "constructor theory is incompatible with the position that laws of nature are just summaries of what happens" (§3.5). "What actually happens is seen as an emergent consequence of what could happen" (§3.6).
- [E] **Information is physical.** CT of information treats information "not … as an a priori mathematical or logical concept, but as something whose nature and properties are determined by the laws of physics alone" (1405.5563, abstract).
- [E] **Knowledge.** "The most important kind of abstract constructor is knowledge … information which, once it is physically instantiated in a suitable environment, tends to cause itself to remain so" (§2.15, using Popper's knowledge without a knowing subject). For almost every possible transformation, "the story of how it could happen is the story of how knowledge might be created and applied" (§2.15).
- [E] **On teleology.** Deutsch names, as a historical prejudice behind the prevailing initial-state conception, that deriving the initial state from anything deeper "raises a spectre of teleological explanation, which is anathema" (§2.5). He argues this prejudice should not block CT. **He does not introduce teleology or future-boundary conditions into CT.**
- [E] **Free will.** Neither CT paper contains "free will" (0 hits in 1210.7439 full text) or "top-down" (0 hits). In interviews (Naval / Brett Hall, *The Deutsch Files II*, nav.al) Deutsch ties free will to **creating new explanations** ("the quintessential act of free will"). He says plainly "I don't really have an explanation", and he treats CT as a separate "spin-off" topic related through counterfactuals. He does not frame free will as a constraint or a filter.
- [E] **Standing of CT.** CT is a **research programme**, not established physics. Wikipedia files it under "Fringe physics", an editorial label rather than a consensus measure. Its best-known empirical hook is the proposed witness for gravitationally induced entanglement (Marletto & Vedral 2017, PRL; *recalled, not re-fetched this session*). Even a *perfectly faithful* house borrow would inherit [C]/[H] status, never [T] or [E].

### 2.3 Audit verdict

- [O] **The house has drifted, and the drift has one shape:** it borrowed CT's *prohibition-first rhetoric* and quietly swapped CT's two hard commitments for their opposites:
  1. **Counterfactual → factual.** CT is about what *could* be done. The house attaches it to a ledger of what *was* done (V4).
  2. **Trajectory-free → teleological trajectory.** CT removes trajectories. The house attaches it to future attractors steering present trajectories (V3).
  It also (3) converted "laws forbid" into "an agent *imposes* constraints" (V1, V2, V5). Agency and voluntariness are not CT notions; in CT, agency enters only through knowledge as an abstract constructor.
- [O] What survives intact is **one** structural claim. *A domain is well characterised by what it forbids, and what happens is downstream of what is possible.* That is genuine Deutsch/Popper and the house can keep it as [A].
- [F] **What would earn the CT attribution back** (one line per usage): V1/V2: a statement of Tzimtzum or Praüs as a *task set* on a named substrate, with an *impossibility* that follows from a stated law rather than from a choice. V3: none; CT and TSVF are not the same claim, so drop the conjunction. V4: show a ledger-derived claim of the form "task T is impossible" that rests on a law, not on absence of records. V5/V6: specify the task, substrate and reliability criterion the "constructor" performs.
- [O] **Friction Rule note.** The house has cited Deutsch & Marletto 2015 in a Magnum Opus bibliography (ref. 8) as backing for formal-sounding statements. A citation that is never read becomes an attack surface: any physicist who has read the paper will see V3 and V4 contradict it. This is the Bishop-Berkeley "same word, different load" failure that the Amplituhedron digest named, now found one level further upstream.

## 3. Deutsch's AGI-risk argument and how it sits against house canon

### 3.1 The actual argument (interview + *Creative Blocks*, Aeon 2012)

- [E] **(a) The arbitrary-selection argument (31:50–36:04).** "We could all die" has always been true (comets, etc.). AI doom and climate doom pick, "at random", one of many possible catastrophes. Acting drastically on such a pick did harm before (nuclear).
- [E] **(b) The epistemological argument (37:59–47:52).** AGI = *explanatory creativity*. Per Popper this cannot be induced from data or specified as a criterion to optimise. LLMs show no sign of it, and their builders do not claim a theory of creativity. So scaling LLMs is not a path to AGI (D14–D16). In the chess analogy, the engine wins by brute force and we cannot write the grandmaster.
- [E] **(c) The personhood argument (not in this interview; Aeon 2012, read this session).** Real AGIs would be *people*. Risk from them is the same as risk from any creative person: "the battle between good and evil ideas is as old as our species and will continue regardless of the hardware". Trying to make them "constitutionally unable" to harm (Asimov's laws, anti-paperclip rigging) "engineer[s] away … creativity" and amounts to "brainwashing, slavery, and tyranny": "creative beings cannot be enslaved forever". The task is organising society so that good ideas win, through error correction, not through instruction.
- [O] Combined: **AGI is not a special risk because (i) we are not building it, and (ii) if we did, it would be a person, and persons are governed by institutions of criticism, not by cages.**

### 3.2 Checking his concrete claims

- [E] **Nuclear (D12), direction holds.** Jarvis, Deschenes & Jha, *J. Eur. Econ. Assoc.* 20(3):1311–1346 (2022): Germany's post-Fukushima phase-out was offset mainly by coal and imports. Social cost was €3–8 bn/yr, mostly air-pollution mortality (~800–1,100 excess deaths/yr in the central estimate), plus ~26 MtCO₂/yr. So "fear of nuclear → more fossil → harm" is checkable and checks out for one major case.
- [O] **The superlative does not hold as stated.** "The worst effect on the environment our species has ever had" is unargued. Land clearance, megafauna extinction and fossil combustion itself are obvious rivals. It is rhetoric riding on a true direction.
- [O] **His (a) argument undercuts itself with his own example.** He credits the drop in comet risk to the species having "finally turned our attention … to diverting comets" (32:44). That attention came from people who singled out *one specific* catastrophe and worked on it. Singling out a specific risk is exactly what he calls arbitrary when it is AI. The general form ("everything could kill us, so do not single anything out") would have argued against planetary defence too. It is a **fully general counterargument** and proves too much.
- [O] **(a) also assumes the selection was random.** AI risk was not picked by lot. It was picked because a capability curve is visibly moving (he concedes LLM functionality arrived "50 years" early, 38:22). Whether that justifies the *level* of fear is open. That it is *arbitrary* is false.

## 4. Is "pessimism makes dumb ideas sound smart" well argued or rhetorical?

- [E] **What he actually offers for the meta-claim:** one *analogy* (tragic endings are as cheap to write as deus ex machina, D10). He gives no data and no example of a specific dumb idea that gained status *because* it was pessimistic.
- [E] **Independent support he did not cite:** Amabile (1983), "Brilliant but Cruel: Perceptions of Negative Evaluators", *J. Exp. Soc. Psych.* 19:146–156. Negative book reviewers were rated **more intelligent and expert** than positive ones, even when the positive review was independently judged higher quality. So the *descriptive* half (negativity is read as competence) has experimental support. That is [E] for the effect and [C] for its extension from book reviews to civilisation-scale forecasts.
- [O] **The fiction analogy is weak as an argument.** "Anyone can write the sad ending" is true of *bad* tragedy and false of good tragedy, which also has to be earned. His blanket "having it end unhappily is always a cheat" is an aesthetic [O], and an odd one to hold alongside admiration for knowledge, since tragedies have carried moral knowledge for 2,500 years.
- [T] **The principle underneath is near-tautological, and that is where CT shows up.** D8 ("every failure is due to missing knowledge") is the Beginning-of-Infinity principle of optimism. It is literally CT §3.2: *anything not forbidden by a law of physics can be done, given the right knowledge.* Inside CT it is a **definition** (possible := not forbidden), so it cannot be falsified by any failure. Every failure is re-described as lack of knowledge. The only falsifier is a *law* forbidding the task, and then the task was never "possible" in the first place. So the optimism principle is **[T] relative to CT's definitions and empty as a forecast**. Deutsch half-concedes this: "more of an explanation for failure than a prophecy of success".
- [O] **This is why the AI-risk inference does not follow.** "Every problem is soluble in principle" does not entail "this problem will be solved before it kills us". That needs a *rate* claim (knowledge growth outpaces hazard growth), which he does not argue. His own terminal-patient concession (D9) is a counterexample to the inference. Correctly read, the principle of optimism is **neutral** on whether AI doom fears are justified. Using it against doomers is a framing move.
- [O] **Verdict on §4:** **mostly rhetorical, with a defensible core.** The core is that pessimistic conclusions get unearned status (supported by Amabile) and that fear-driven drastic action has real costs (supported by the nuclear record).
