## 1. Debate structure & core positions

The debate follows a formal timed structure: 5-minute opening statements, 5-minute cross-examinations, 3-minute rebuttals, 3-minute closing statements, followed by ~25 minutes of open moderated dialogue.

- **Martin Ford's Thesis (The Discontinuity / Displacement Stance):**  
  AI is historically unique because it is the first technology to compete directly with general human cognitive capability. While immediate short-term job loss has been muted (except in entry-level white-collar and junior coding roles), rapid advances in 2026 (agents, autonomous math proofs) combined with eventual breakthroughs in continual learning will allow machines to substitute for at least 50% of the labor force within 20 years. Ford relies centrally on his **Horse Analogy**: the railroad initially complemented horses (causing horse populations to peak around World War I), but the internal combustion engine rendered them obsolete; AI will do to human cognitive labor what the internal combustion engine did to equine muscle. Because capitalism's profit-maximization imperative provides no incentive to retain unnecessary workers, aggregate consumer demand will collapse unless structural reforms (such as a Universal Basic Income / guaranteed minimum income) are implemented.
- **Pedro Domingos's Thesis (The Continuity / Economic Expansion Stance):**  
  AI is simply the next wave in a 200-year history of automation, and every previous wave (steam, electricity, computers, ATMs) created more jobs than it destroyed. Jobs are bundles of diverse tasks; automating some tasks lowers the cost of production, increases productivity, and—via **Jevons paradox** and standard microeconomic complementarity—raises the value and demand for the remaining human labor. Furthermore, money saved by consumers on cheaper goods is spent on other services, expanding overall employment. On the technical side, human general intelligence is nowhere near being cracked; the brain's 20-watt efficiency outperforms data centers by six orders of magnitude; modern machine learning lacks broad generalization and transfer learning outside data-rich, formally verifiable domains (math and coding); and AGI is at least 100 years away (give or take an order of magnitude). Alarmism about job loss distracts from real risks (stupid AI making errors, bad actors, losing the technological competition to China).

## 3. The Central Divergence: Technology vs. Economics

The debate crystallizes around an irreconcilable meta-theoretical split:

1. **Martin Ford treats the problem as TECHNOLOGICAL DETERMINISM:**  
   If an algorithm can match or exceed human cognitive capability across broad domains, human labor loses its economic scarcity value. The market mechanism has no intrinsic loyalty to human livelihood; firms face fiduciary mandates to cut costs. Therefore, labor displacement is a mechanical consequence of software capability.
2. **Pedro Domingos treats the problem as ECONOMIC GENERAL EQUILIBRIUM:**  
   Technology operates strictly within macroeconomic laws. Labor is not an isolated cost item; it is the complement to capital, and workers are the ultimate source of demand. Lowering the cost of cognitive tasks liberates purchasing power to consume new goods and services. Because human wants are non-satiating ("infinite demand"), employment shifts dynamically into newly created categories, as it has across every technological revolution since 1800.
