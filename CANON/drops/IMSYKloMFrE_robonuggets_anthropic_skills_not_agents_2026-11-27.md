## §3. The title, checked against the primary

The title is **not a fabrication**. T1's own slide says *Stop Building Agents* and its closing line is «it's time to stop rebuilding agents and start building skills instead.» A creator compressing that into a title has a primary to point at.

It is still **OVERSTATED**, for three reasons that compound:

1. **The qualifier is dropped.** T1's object is the *rebuild per domain*: «building a separate agent with custom scaffolding for every domain». The tape body keeps that qualifier (for every single task). The title does not. *Stopped building agents* and *stopped rebuilding one bespoke agent per task* are different claims.
2. **A team's practice becomes a company's policy.** The *we* in T1 is two people on the skills team presenting a design argument. *Anthropic's Engineers* reads as a workforce-wide stop. No Anthropic primary says the company stopped building agents.
3. **Anthropic's own Claude Code lead describes the opposite at runtime.** In the same period, Cherny describes dynamic workflows that let a team «orchestrate, you know, dozens, hundreds, thousands of agents to do work productively», and says the team runs hundreds of agents a day, sometimes thousands. That is not a company that stopped building agents; it is a company that stopped shipping a separate agent product for every use case while spawning many agents inside one product. The distinction is real and the title erases it.

**Title verdict:** OVERSTATED. The body is closer to the primary than the title is. Anyone citing this video should cite the body sentence, not the title.

## §4. Verdict — strongest point and weakest joint

**★ STRONGEST: the architectural spine is the primary's spine, and the video does not bend it.**

C2, C3, C4, C6, C7 and C14 are not lucky guesses; they are contiguous matches to T1, B1 and B3, including the exact three platform names. The chip/OS/apps analogy is T1's closing slide, and the video uses it the way T1 uses it: to argue that the scarce input is procedural expertise, not a new agent architecture. That is the best thing in the video, and it is also the least original thing in it.

The second strength is C20. The six-month deletion advice is the most contrarian line on the tape, and it is literally sourced, down to `hooks` — a detail that the Podhood chapter summary and the Transcripts.wiki summary both drop. A creator who reproduces the awkward detail rather than the tidy summary is doing something right. The video is at its best where it is transcribing.

**★ WEAKEST: C8, the definition of the harness as a set of system prompts.**

This is not a small terminology slip, because the whole contrast depends on it. On the video's account the stack is: model (chip), harness (system prompts), skills (folders). If the harness is just prompt text, then *skills versus agents* collapses into *folders versus prompts*, and the level-six advice becomes *delete the OS to see what the chip can do* — which is not what Cherny describes and not what Anthropic documents.

Anthropic's own glossary defines the harness as «The tools, context management, and execution environment that turn a language model into a capable coding agent», and spells out «file access, shell execution, permission gating, memory loading, and the loop that chains actions together.» Those are not system prompts. The 80 percent cut in C21 was a cut to one component of the harness for one model; the loop, the tools and the permission gate stayed. The video's frame makes the cut sound like evidence that the harness is disposable, when the primary describes an ablation of stale corrections inside a harness that remains.

The consequence is practical: a viewer who takes C8 seriously will model their own stack wrongly, then over-generalise C20 into *delete the scaffolding*. Anthropic's own harness article says the opposite: «carefully set boundaries with the agent harness.» The video has the right advice attached to the wrong layer.

**Runner-up weakest: C19.** Delegating a security audit to the agent that will execute the skill is the single most dangerous sentence on the tape, and it is presented as the safe practice. The primary tells the person to read the files. See §5, R6.
