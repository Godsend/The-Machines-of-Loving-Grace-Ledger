## §4 · Verdict

**Strongest point.** **Walzer's condition, delivered as a synthesis rather than a compromise** — *"we don't want someone who can just make that deal… What we actually want is someone who can make that deal, and they can feel bad about it."* It is the episode's one genuinely load-bearing idea and it is stated in a form the house can use verbatim: it separates **the act** from **the account of the act**, and it makes the account non-optional. Runner-up: **the agency reading of lying** (R3), which arrives from moral philosophy at the house's register rule and is the cleanest external restatement of "distorting information is the harm".

**Weakest joint.** **The Emerson pivot, and it is weak in a specific way.** The episode promotes power-dependence from a *descriptive* theory of relationship structure into *"the missing keystone"* that resolves a normative dilemma, and then extracts a **licence** (*"rational for the person with less power to be a little bit manipulative"*) that no evidence on the tape supports and that contradicts its own better argument nine minutes later (R6). The empirical base named for the pivot — *"one estimate had it at 70 to 75% of relationships are generally equally powered"* — is **uncited, unnamed, and not verifiable from the tape** [C]. **Why this belongs in canon.** It is a **widely-consumed, dated statement of the manipulation question from the popular self-help lane** — the exact register in which the house's own provenance discipline has to survive popularisation — and it contains, in one artifact, both the house-compatible structural answer (**R6**) and the house-hostile licence (**R2**).
